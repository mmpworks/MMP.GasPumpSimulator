One perfect sale, piece by piece

A driver taps a credit card, types a ZIP code, picks Regular, fills up, stops to take a phone call, finishes, and hangs up the nozzle. Nothing goes wrong. This article follows that one sale through the software. For each part it says what the part decides and who it talks to next. Read this one first. Every other path through the code is this path with a wrong turn in it.

Station marks the computer inside the store that owns the sale and the money. Dispenser marks the computer out on the island that owns the valve and the meter.

1. Six words we use

Think of a small gas station run by three people:

  • One sits at the register and keeps the sales book.
  • One sits next to them with a phone and calls the bank.
  • One is out on the island, running the pump.

They never shout across the room. They pass each other slips of paper. That is the whole design, and these six words describe it.

Worker
One of the three people. In the software a worker is a loop. It waits for a slip, handles it, and waits for the next one. It handles one slip at a time, so it never trips over itself. The three workers are:
  • the sale worker, the sales book,
  • the payment worker, the phone to the bank,
  • the pump worker, the hands on the pump.
Mailbox
Each worker's in-tray. Anyone can drop a slip in. Only the owner takes slips out, in the order they arrived. A tray has a size. When a tray fills up, one of two things happens:
  • the sender waits, or
  • the slip is dropped and counted.
Either way, one slow worker cannot drown the others. In the code a mailbox is a bounded queue (a line with a size limit). _saleBox.Post(...) drops a slip into the sale worker's tray.
Message
The slip itself. Every slip is a small record with a name on top, like CardPresented, Arm, Progress, or NozzleHung. Slips come in three stacks:
  • SaleEvent: things that happened to the sale,
  • PumpMessage: things to do at the pump,
  • PaymentMessage: things to ask the bank.
A slip is just a record. So the same slip can go across a room or across a cable without being rewritten.
Wire
The cable between the store and the island.
  • The sale and payment workers live in one program, GasPump.Host.exe, inside the store.
  • The pump worker lives in a second program, GasPump.Dispenser.exe, out on the island.
  • The wire is a TCP connection between them on port 5178. It carries one slip per line of text, written as JSON.
  • Pump messages go out. Sale events, pump views, and audit lines come back.
Token and nonce
Three pieces make one permission slip:
  • The token is the slip the station signs for the pump: "this sale may pump up to $30 and 50 gallons, until this time."
  • The seal is a signature made with a secret key both sides hold.
  • The nonce is a "number used once", a random number stamped on each slip.
The pump writes down every nonce it has seen. If the same one arrives again, the slip is a copy of an old one. The pump refuses it even though the seal is good. That is how a replayed token dies.
Ticker
A metronome on the island. Every 100 milliseconds it drops a Tick slip in the pump worker's tray. The pump worker uses each tick to read the meter and report how much fuel moved. Nothing on the store side ticks. The store just hears the reports.

How the three workers run on the CPU is a choice made once at startup. There are three choices:

  • one thread each,
  • Tasks over channels,
  • the AsyncProxy iterator machines.

The choice changes nothing in this story. The tests run the whole sale three times, once per choice, and the trace below comes out the same each time.

2. The dispenser, out on the island

Dispenser The island computer runs one worker, the pump worker, and that worker has the most hands-on job. It is the mechanic. It owns every physical part that touches fuel, and it keeps the records that prove how much fuel moved.

The pump worker has six jobs.

  • Checking the permission slip. The station sends a signed token that says "this sale may pump up to $30 and 50 gallons." The authority runs four checks:
    • the signature is good,
    • the token names this pump,
    • the token has not expired,
    • the token has never been used before.
    Then it clamps. If the token asks for more than the pump's own hard limits ($500, 50 gallons), the pump lowers it. The station can be wrong or hacked. The pump still pumps only what it allows itself.
  • The four interlocks. Fuel moves only when all four are true at once:
    • the pump is armed,
    • a grade is picked,
    • the nozzle is off its hook,
    • the spout is inserted in the fill pipe (the bellows interlock).
    The lever is the fifth thing. Squeeze it with all four true and the valve opens. Any one false and the squeeze is written down and ignored.
  • The valve. A solenoid (an electric switch) opens and closes the fuel line.
    • At the start of a sale the worker opens it all the way.
    • Near the end it opens the valve only part way. The last few cents dribble in, and the sale lands on the ceiling to the penny.
    • That slowdown is the taper.
  • The meter and the totalizer.
    • The meter is a pulser: a little wheel in the fuel line that clicks a fixed number of times per gallon.
    • On every tick the worker counts the clicks. It turns them into gallons, then into dollars using the price board.
    • The totalizer is a lifetime counter of every gallon that ever left this hose. It never resets. Auditors read it.
  • Watching for trouble. On every tick the worker checks four things:
    • the flow switch: valve open but nothing moving means the tank is dry,
    • the pulser: fuel moving but no clicks means a broken meter,
    • the breakaway coupling: a car drove off with the hose,
    • the click count: more clicks than fuel could have moved means someone is faking the meter.
    Any of these stops the valve at once and names the part that failed.
  • Keeping its own records, and sending a copy out. The pump keeps three records:
    • the ledger: every arm, every valve close, every fault. It is a hash chain, so each line locks in the one before it.
    • the custody file: the same lines, in a form the station can verify.
    • the state file: the totalizer and the list of used tokens. It survives a power cut.
    The custody file is written and sent at the same time. The instant a line lands on the island's disk, the same bytes go down the wire to the station, which appends them to its own copy and checks them with the same key. That gives two copies of the whole transaction, in two places:
    • lose the island, and the station still has the chain;
    • lose the station, and the island still has it;
    • when the wire comes back, the island replays the chain and the station's copy keeps only the lines it was missing.

Notice what the pump worker does not know:

  • what a sale is,
  • who the driver is,
  • what card they used.

It knows a token, a valve, and a counter. That is on purpose. A small program that handles money is easier to trust than a big one that also handles fuel. The same goes for a small program that handles fuel.

3. The station, inside the store

Station The store computer runs the other two workers and holds everything about money.

The sale worker is the person at the register with the sales book. The book is the SaleStateMachine. It has one open sale at a time. For that sale it tracks two things, apart from each other:

  • Where the pump is (SaleState). The pump is in one of five spots:
    • waiting for a ZIP,
    • asking the bank,
    • ready to pump,
    • fueling,
    • finishing.
  • Where the money is (HoldStatus). The money is in one of five spots:
    • nothing asked,
    • hold requested,
    • held,
    • settled,
    • released.

The two drift apart when the bank is slow. A driver who was charged and got no gas lives in that gap. Keeping both columns keeps the book straight.

The book has one odd rule: it never does anything itself. Hand it a slip. It writes in the book, then hands back a list of chores. For example:

  • "start a 30 second timer",
  • "ask the bank for a hold",
  • "tell the pump to arm",
  • "send the bank the final amount".

The sale worker then does the chores by dropping slips in other trays. Because of this split, the book can be tested with an ordinary list and no threads.

The payment worker is the person with the phone to the bank. Its tool is the PaymentGateway. A call goes through three steps:

  1. The gateway does the checks a terminal can do alone: is the card number well formed, is it expired.
  2. The call goes through a network simulator. It can add delay, drop the line, or answer late.
  3. The call reaches the issuer, an in-memory bank. It keeps accounts, balances, a PIN or a billing ZIP on file, and a list of open holds.

The payment worker returns no value when the bank answers. It drops a slip back in the sale worker's tray: Approved, Declined, or CompletionSent.

Around the two workers sit the things a store keeps:

  • the site limits: how much to hold on a credit or debit card. An admin PIN changes them.
  • the cash drawer.
  • the journal: it snapshots the whole sale to disk every second, so a power cut can be recovered.
  • the station's own ledger and audit chain.
  • a copy of the dispenser's chain.
  • the capability listeners: side programs that hear every sale event but can never write back. The trace in the next section comes from one of them.

5. Tap: a card becomes a sale

Short version. The driver taps a card. The station opens a new page in the sales book and starts a 30 second clock. The bank waits. The screen asks for a ZIP code.

Station The Tap button sends a web request to /api/card. The host hands it to Simulation.TryPresentCard. That method first asks BeginSale whether the pump will take a new payment at all. Three things can make it say no:

  • the pump is out of service,
  • fuel is moving,
  • someone else's approved sale has a nozzle in hand.

None of those is true. So a fresh SaleStateMachine gets a new id, and the card slip goes in the sale worker's tray.

// Simulation.cs
public (bool Ok, string SaleId, string Reason) TryPresentCard(string cardName, Grade? grade = null, Tender tender = Tender.Credit, CardSource source = CardSource.PumpInsert)
{
    if (tender == Tender.Cash) throw new ArgumentException("use PayCash for a cash sale", nameof(tender));
    var (ok, id, reason) = BeginSale($"new card {cardName} {SaleStateMachine.SourceText(source)}");
    if (!ok) return (false, id, reason);
    _saleBox.Post(new SaleEvent.CardPresented(id, cardName, grade, tender, source));
    return (true, id, "");
}

The sale worker's whole job is this one handler. It runs the same twenty lines for every slip in this article:

  1. Take a slip.
  2. Write it in the book.
  3. Do the chores the book hands back.
  4. Update the screen.
  5. Tell the listeners.
// Simulation.cs
private void HandleSaleEvent(SaleEvent e)
{
    IReadOnlyList<SaleCommand> commands;
    SaleState before, after;
    SaleSnapshot snap;
    lock (_gate)
    {
        if (e.SaleId != _sale.SaleId) { HandleOrphanEvent(e); return; }
        before = _sale.State;
        commands = _sale.Apply(e);
        after = _sale.State;
        snap = _sale.Snapshot();
    }
    foreach (var c in commands) Execute(c);
    RecordSaleBoundary(e, before, after, snap);
    Publish();
    Capabilities.Publish(e);
}

Inside the book, a card in Idle is written down. The book records three things:

  • which card,
  • credit or debit,
  • tapped or inserted.

The page then moves to CardRead with a 30 second timer. Nothing goes to the bank. The card alone buys the driver a prompt and a countdown.

// SaleStateMachine.cs
case (SaleState.Idle, SaleEvent.CardPresented cp):
    CardName = cp.CardName;
    Grade = cp.Grade ?? Grade.Regular;
    GradeSelected = cp.Grade is not null;
    Tender = cp.Tender;
    Source = cp.Source;
    // Nothing has gone to the bank yet. The keypad comes first: a PIN for debit, a ZIP code for credit.
    return Transition(SaleState.CardRead, commands, $"card {cp.CardName} as {cp.Tender}, {SourceText(cp.Source)}, waiting for {CodeFor(cp.Tender).Name}", timeout: _timeouts.CodeEntry);

Two chores come back:

  1. Cancel any old timer.
  2. Start a 30 second timer.

The screen reads "Enter your 5-digit billing ZIP code" with the countdown. The station ledger gets a sale.open line.

6. Keypad: the ZIP goes in

Short version. The driver types 12345. The book checks that it has five digits and nothing else. If it does, the book writes "asked the bank" and hands the payment worker a slip with the card and the ZIP on it.

Station Enter sends /api/code. That becomes Simulation.EnterCode, which drops a CodeEntered slip in the sale tray. The book checks the shape:

  • a credit card wants five digits,
  • a debit card wants four.

Anything else is refused. The log line says how many digits were typed. It never says which ones.

// SaleStateMachine.cs
case (SaleState.CardRead, SaleEvent.CodeEntered ce):
{
    var (digits, name, _) = CodeFor(Tender);
    if (ce.Code.Length != digits || !ce.Code.All(char.IsDigit))
    {
        // The digits never reach a log. Only the shape of the mistake does.
        commands.Add(new SaleCommand.Log(SaleId, $"{name} entry refused: {digits} digits expected, {ce.Code.Length} typed; waiting"));
        return commands;
    }
    Hold = HoldStatus.Requested;
    commands.Add(new SaleCommand.RequestAuthorization(SaleId, CardName!, Grade, Tender, ce.Code));
    return Transition(SaleState.Authorizing, commands, $"{name} entered, asking the bank", timeout: _timeouts.Authorization);
}

Three things happen:

  • The money column flips to Requested.
  • The page moves to Authorizing with a 30 second timer.
  • A RequestAuthorization chore carries the ZIP out.

Execute looks up the test card by name and puts the ZIP beside it. Then it drops the slip in the payment worker's tray.

// Simulation.cs, inside Execute
case SaleCommand.RequestAuthorization ra:
    var card = TestCards.ByName.TryGetValue(ra.CardName, out var known) ? known : new CardData("0000000000000000", 1, 2000, CardKind.Credit);
    _paymentBox.Post(new PaymentMessage.PreAuthorize(ra.SaleId, card, ra.Tender, ra.Code));
    break;

Why the book checks the shape and the bank checks the value. The book talks to nothing outside itself, so it can only know what it was handed. A PIN is a secret between the driver and their bank. A ZIP is a fact only the bank has on file. So the bank does the real check. That keeps the book's claims inside what it can know. It also keeps the digits out of every line the book writes.

7. Bank: the hold and the token

Short version. The payment worker asks the bank to set aside $30. The bank checks the card and the ZIP, then says yes. The payment worker signs a permission slip (the token) for $30. It drops an "Approved" slip back in the sale tray.

Station The payment worker's handler is the one place where the worker style shows on purpose. The style changes how the call to the bank waits:

  • With one thread per worker, the call just blocks. This worker has its own thread, so nobody is waiting on it.
  • With Tasks or AsyncProxy, the call carries on in the background. The worker goes back to its tray.

Either way the answer comes back as a slip.

// Simulation.cs
private void HandlePaymentMessage(PaymentMessage m)
{
    Interlocked.Increment(ref _inflightPayments);
    var work = RunPaymentAsync(m);
    if (_dispatch.PrefersBlockingIo)
    {
        // The 2008 way: this worker has its own thread, and the host call blocks it. Nobody else waits.
        work.GetAwaiter().GetResult();
    }
    else
    {
        // The modern way: let the call continue on a callback. The worker moves on. Results post back as events.
        _ = work;
    }
}

PaymentGateway.PreAuthorizeAsync first does two checks a terminal can do without calling anyone:

  • the card number's checksum,
  • the expiry date.

Then it decides what the typed code means. For debit it is the PIN. For credit it is the ZIP. The amount it asks the bank to hold is the site's ceiling for that kind of card. That is $30 in the trace, because the test set it there.

// PaymentGateway.cs
var check = CardValidator.Check(card, now);
if (check != CardCheck.Ok)
    return new PreAuthResult(false, Money.Zero, null, check.ToString(), LocalDeclineMessage(check), NetworkFailure: false);

// The tender decides what the typed code means. Debit sends it as the PIN; a debit sale with no PIN is a PIN failure
// at the bank, which is what a real terminal sees. Credit sends it as the ZIP for the address check.
var presented = tender == Tender.Debit
    ? card with { Kind = CardKind.Debit, Pin = keypad }
    : card with { Zip = keypad };
var requested = Site.HoldFor(tender);

var result = await _network.SendAsync(() => _issuer.Authorize(saleId, presented, requested, now), _options.HostTimeout, ct);

The call goes through the network simulator, which today is up with 50 ms of delay. The bank walks its checks in a fixed order:

  1. Is this sale already voided or held?
  2. Does the account exist?
  3. Is the card lost or frozen?
  4. Does the PIN match? (debit only)
  5. Does the ZIP match? (credit only)
  6. Has the card been used too often?
  7. Is there any money?

Check 5, the ZIP check, is the new one.

// Issuer.cs
if (card.Kind == CardKind.Debit && (card.Pin is null || card.Pin != acct.Pin))
    return IssuerDecision.Decline(DeclineReason.InvalidPin, "invalid PIN");
// Credit runs the address check: the ZIP typed at the pump against the billing ZIP on file.
if (card.Kind == CardKind.Credit && acct.Zip is not null && card.Zip != acct.Zip)
    return IssuerDecision.Decline(DeclineReason.ZipMismatch, "billing ZIP does not match");
...
// Fuel is MCC 5542, and partial approval is standard for it.
var granted = Money.Min(hold, acct.Available);
acct.Available -= granted;
_holds[saleId] = new Hold(card.Pan, granted);

12345 matches. The bank takes $30 off the balance and holds it under the sale id.

Back at the gateway, the pump's ceiling is the smaller of two numbers: what the bank granted, and what the store allows. (A manager may have lowered the limit while the call was out.)

The gateway then signs a token. The token holds six things:

  • the sale id,
  • the pump id,
  • the ceiling,
  • the max gallons,
  • how long it is good for,
  • a one-time number called a nonce.

The gateway signs all of it with a secret key the dispenser also holds. Think of the token as a permission slip with a wax seal. The payment worker drops Approved(ceiling, token) in the sale tray.

8. Arm: the token crosses the wire

Short version. The book writes "money is held" and sends the sealed slip across the cable. The pump worker checks the seal, the pump, and the date. It lowers the amount if the slip asks for too much. Then it reports "armed for $30".

Station Approved arriving while the page says Authorizing is the moment the money becomes real. The book does four things:

  1. It records the ceiling.
  2. It keeps the token.
  3. It marks the hold Held.
  4. It asks for the pump to be armed.

The page moves to ReadyToPump with a 120 second timer. The timer is long because a card read at the pump means the driver is standing right there, and nobody else can cancel for them.

// SaleStateMachine.cs
case (SaleState.Authorizing, SaleEvent.Approved a):
    Ceiling = a.Ceiling;
    Token = a.Token;
    Hold = HoldStatus.Held;
    commands.Add(new SaleCommand.ArmPump(SaleId, a.Token, GradeSelected ? Grade : null));
    return Transition(SaleState.ReadyToPump, commands, $"approved, ceiling {a.Ceiling}, {LiftTimeout.TotalSeconds:0} s to lift", timeout: LiftTimeout);

Execute turns the ArmPump chore into a PumpMessage.Arm slip and hands it to the link. The link comes in two forms:

  • With both halves in one program, the link is the pump worker's tray.
  • With two programs, the link is SocketDispenserLink. It writes the slip as one line of JSON down the cable.

The slip is the same either way. That is why the pump could move out to its own program without rewriting a single message.

Dispenser On the island, Dispenser.Handle is the pump worker's handler. It is a switch with one case per kind of slip. Every case ends by sending zero or more sale events back up the cable.

// Dispenser.cs
case PumpMessage.Arm arm:
    var (ok, reason, armed) = Pump.Arm(arm.SaleId, arm.Token, arm.Grade);
    if (ok) { Log.Write("pump", "armed {Ceiling} max {MaxVolume} ({ClampNote})", ("Ceiling", armed!.Ceiling), ("MaxVolume", armed.MaxVolume), ("ClampNote", armed.ClampNote)); events.Add(new SaleEvent.PumpArmed(arm.SaleId, armed.Ceiling, armed.MaxVolume)); }
    else { Log.Warn("pump", "REFUSED arm for {SaleId}: {Reason}", ("SaleId", arm.SaleId), ("Reason", reason)); events.Add(new SaleEvent.PumpRefused(arm.SaleId, reason)); }
    break;

PumpController.Arm says no if fuel is already moving or a part is broken. Otherwise it hands the token to the authority. The authority does two things, always in this order:

  1. Verify. It checks four things:
    • the seal (the signature) is good,
    • the slip names this pump,
    • the slip is inside its lifetime,
    • the one-time number has never been seen here before.
  2. Clamp. It takes the dollars and gallons on the slip. If the slip asked for more than the pump's own limits ($500 and 50 gallons), it pulls them down. A $30 slip needs no clamping.

Then the controller does three things:

  • it zeroes the meter for this sale,
  • it makes sure the valve is shut,
  • it reports Armed.
// PumpController.cs
var (armed, refusal) = authority.TryArm(saleId, tokenWire);
if (armed is null) return (false, refusal, null);

_armed = armed;
_armedAt = clock.GetUtcNow();
_grade = preselectedGrade;
_poured = false;
_leverHeld = false;
...
meter.ResetSale();
hydraulics.Close();
foreach (var stage in _stages) stage.OnArmed(saleId);
Phase = PumpPhase.Armed;
return (true, "", armed);

PumpArmed goes back up the cable. The book notes the ceiling and writes "armed" in its phase column. That is the ReadyToPump/idle > ReadyToPump/armed pair in the trace. First the bank said yes. Then the pump said it heard. The screen reads "Approved for up to $30.00. Select a grade."

9. Hands: grade, lift, insert, squeeze

Short version. The driver picks Regular, lifts the nozzle, puts it in the tank, and squeezes. Each of the first three flips one switch. The squeeze checks all the switches and opens the valve. From then on the metronome reports gallons and dollars ten times a second.

Dispenser Four buttons, four slips, four switches in the controller:

  • SelectGrade picks the price.
  • Lift is the hook switch.
  • InsertSpout is the bellows interlock: the spout is in the fill pipe.
  • Squeeze is the lever. The next paragraph covers it.

The first three each make the pump worker answer with a matching sale event. The book moves the screen's hint along: "Lift the nozzle", then "Put the nozzle in your tank", then "Squeeze the lever to begin". These three open nothing.

Squeeze is where the interlocks live. All four must pass. If one fails, the squeeze is written in the ledger as ignored and nothing happens. The first squeeze of a sale is the first pour, and that starts fueling. Lifting the nozzle never starts it.

// PumpController.cs
public (bool Ok, string Reason, bool FirstPour) SqueezeLever()
{
    _leverHeld = true;
    if (_armed is null || Phase is not (PumpPhase.Armed or PumpPhase.Paused))
    {
        ledger.Append("lever.ignored", $"phase {Phase}");
        return (false, $"not armed ({Phase})", false);
    }
    if (_grade is null) { ledger.Append("lever.ignored", $"{_armed.SaleId} no grade selected"); return (false, "select a grade first", false); }
    if (!_nozzleLifted) { ledger.Append("lever.ignored", $"{_armed.SaleId} nozzle in boot"); return (false, "lift the nozzle first", false); }
    if (!_spoutInserted) { ledger.Append("lever.ignored", $"{_armed.SaleId} spout not in the fill pipe"); return (false, "insert the spout first", false); }

    var first = !_poured;
    _poured = true;
    Phase = PumpPhase.Dispensing;
    _noFlowFor = TimeSpan.Zero;
    hydraulics.SetOpening(1);
    ...

A first pour becomes FlowStarted. Any later squeeze becomes FlowResumed. Station The book takes FlowStarted and moves the page to Fueling with a ten-minute timer. From here the hands go quiet and the ticker does the talking.

Dispenser Every 100 ms the ticker drops a Tick in the pump worker's tray. PumpController.Tick then does six things:

  1. It asks the hydraulics how much fuel moved.
  2. It asks the meter how many clicks it counted.
  3. It checks that the two agree.
  4. It adds the gallons to the totalizer.
  5. It prices the slice off the price board.
  6. It starts closing the valve as the money nears the ceiling.

The result is one PumpTick. The pump worker sends it up as Progress.

// Dispenser.cs, inside the Tick case
events.Add(new SaleEvent.Progress(saleId, t.Volume, t.Money, t.Flow, t.TaperPhase, t.Lines));

Station The book copies five things from each Progress slip into the page:

  • gallons,
  • dollars,
  • flow,
  • phase,
  • the itemized lines.

It hands back no chores at all. A hundred of these slips is the "100 Progress events" line in the trace. Each one refreshes the screen's big numbers.

// SaleStateMachine.cs
case (SaleState.Fueling, SaleEvent.Progress p):
    Volume = p.Volume;
    Money = p.Money;
    if (p.Lines is not null) Lines = p.Lines;
    Flow = p.Flow;
    Phase = p.Phase;
    return commands;

10. The phone call

Short version. The phone rings. The driver lets go of the lever. The valve shuts and a 60 second clock starts. Ten seconds later the driver squeezes again. The clock is cancelled and fuel moves again. A call longer than a minute would have ended the sale by itself, for the fuel already poured.

Dispenser Five seconds in, the driver lets go. Release reaches the controller. The controller does three things:

  1. It shuts the valve.
  2. It moves to Paused.
  3. It answers FlowPaused("lever released").

Pulling the nozzle out of the tank does the same with a different reason.

Station The book zeroes the flow, writes "paused", and starts a second timer. This one is tagged idle. It runs 60 seconds beside the ten-minute timer already running. Two timers can be live on one sale, which is why timers carry a tag. A state change cancels all of them. A resume cancels only this one.

// SaleStateMachine.cs
case (SaleState.Fueling, SaleEvent.FlowPaused fp):
    Flow = FlowRate.Zero;
    Phase = "paused";
    // The idle timer runs beside the max-fueling timer. A squeeze cancels it; silence lets it finish the sale.
    commands.Add(new SaleCommand.ArmTimer(SaleId, SaleState.Fueling, _timeouts.PausedFlow, SaleCommand.IdleTimer));
    commands.Add(new SaleCommand.Log(SaleId, $"flow paused: {fp.Reason}, {Volume} for {Money} so far, finishes in {_timeouts.PausedFlow.TotalSeconds:0} s without a squeeze"));
    return commands;

case (SaleState.Fueling, SaleEvent.FlowResumed):
    Phase = "full";
    commands.Add(new SaleCommand.CancelTimer(SaleId, SaleCommand.IdleTimer));
    commands.Add(new SaleCommand.Log(SaleId, "flow resumed"));
    return commands;

The screen shows the money so far and the words "Squeeze the lever to continue. Hang up the nozzle to finish, or the pump finishes by itself after 60 s". The countdown switches to the idle clock because it is the nearer one. The driver talks for ten seconds. The meter stands still. The test checks that the gallons at the end of the call equal the gallons at the start.

Call over, one squeeze. The controller is in Paused, so the interlocks pass and the valve opens again. Three things follow:

  • FlowResumed cancels the idle clock.
  • The phase goes back to "full", and the countdown goes back to the ten-minute one.
  • Progress slips start again from where the money left off.

If the call had run long. At 60 seconds the idle timer fires a Timeout tagged idle. The book closes the valve and finishes the sale for the fuel delivered, with the reason NoFlow. A nozzle forgotten in a tank never holds a pump open.

Say a squeeze and the timer cross in the tray. The timer arrives with the phase already "full", so it is logged and ignored. PausedFlowTests walks both cases.

11. Hang up: settling the bill

Short version. The driver hangs up the nozzle. The pump shuts the valve and says "done, 1.7 gallons, $5.95." The book first writes down, on disk, what it is about to tell the bank. Then it tells the bank. The bank keeps $5.95 and gives back the other $24.05. The screen says thank you.

Dispenser PumpController.HangNozzle drops the three nozzle switches. Fuel was poured, so it calls Stop. Stop does three things:

  • It shuts the valve.
  • It writes a valve.close line in the dispenser's ledger, with gallons, dollars, and the totalizer reading.
  • It clears the sale from the controller.

The pump rests at Stopped. Every finished delivery ends there, and the next Arm picks up from there. The pump worker answers NozzleHung.

Station NozzleHung while fueling ends the delivery. The book asks for the valve to close. (It is already shut, and the pump ignores a second close.) Then the book calls Finish. Finish has two branches:

  • No fuel moved: it reverses the hold instead of charging, because a charge for zero is wrong.
  • Fuel moved: it sends the bank the final amount with its itemized lines. It moves the page to Completing with a 30 second timer.

Fuel did move, so this sale takes the second branch.

// SaleStateMachine.cs
case (SaleState.Fueling, SaleEvent.NozzleHung):
    Flow = FlowRate.Zero;
    Phase = "hung";
    NozzleOut = false;
    SpoutInserted = false;
    commands.Add(new SaleCommand.CloseValve(SaleId));
    return Finish(commands, "nozzle hung", EndReason.Completed);

private IReadOnlyList<SaleCommand> Finish(List<SaleCommand> commands, string why, EndReason end)
{
    End = end;
    if (Volume == Volume.Zero)
    {
        // No fuel moved. A completion for zero is wrong; the hold must be reversed in full.
        End = EndReason.DriverCancelled;
        EndDetail = "no fuel was dispensed";
        return Transition(SaleState.Cancelled, commands, $"{why}, no fuel, hold reversed", reverse: true);
    }
    commands.Add(new SaleCommand.SendCompletion(SaleId, Money, Charges));
    return Transition(SaleState.Completing, commands, $"{why}, {Volume} for {Money}", timeout: _timeouts.Completion);
}

Execute does something here that it does nowhere else on this path: it writes before it sends. The final amount is money owed to the bank. So the intent goes into the gateway's pending list first, and the journal saves that list to disk. Only then does the payment worker see the slip. If the power dies in the next millisecond, the restart finds the intent and sends it.

// Simulation.cs, inside Execute
// Money owed to the bank is written to the compensation log here, on the deciding thread, before the
// payment worker ever sees the message. A crash between this line and the bank's answer is recoverable.
case SaleCommand.SendCompletion sc:
    Gateway.Intend(new PendingAdvice(sc.SaleId, PendingKind.Completion, sc.Final, sc.Lines));
    _paymentBox.Post(new PaymentMessage.Complete(sc.SaleId, sc.Final, sc.Lines));
    break;

The payment worker calls CompleteAsync. It records the same intent again. (A second copy of the same sale and kind changes nothing.) Then it sends everything in the pending list, oldest first, down the one path all bank messages use.

The bank's Complete does four things:

  • It removes the $30 hold.
  • It keeps $5.95.
  • It puts $24.05 back on the balance.
  • It files the settlement with its two charge lines for the statement.

The gateway sees its slip in the sent list and reports success.

// PaymentGateway.cs
public async Task<CompletionResult> CompleteAsync(string saleId, Money final, IReadOnlyList<ChargeLine>? lines, CancellationToken ct)
{
    ...
    // Intent first, then one send path for everything owed, oldest first. If an older advice fails, this one waits behind it.
    var advice = new PendingAdvice(saleId, PendingKind.Completion, final, lines);
    Intend(advice);
    var sent = await DrainPendingAsync(ct);
    return sent.Any(advice.SameAs)
        ? new CompletionResult(true, false, $"completion {final} sent")
        : new CompletionResult(false, true, $"host {LastOutcome}, completion {final} queued");
}

CompletionSent goes back in the sale tray. Money slips are legal in every state, so the book handles this one in its money table before the state switch. In Completing it marks the hold Settled and moves to Idle. Then:

  • The screen reads "Thank you: 1.700 gal for $5.95. Your card was charged $5.95."
  • The station ledger gets its sale.close line.
  • The receipt is the page's charge lines.

The screen stays up until the next card.

12. What stayed quiet

Half the sales book is for days that go worse than this one. None of it ran today, and knowing it is there is part of knowing the path.

  • No reversal. A reversal gives a hold back when no fuel came out. It covers three cases:
    • a decline after the hold,
    • a walk-away before the lift,
    • a cancel.
    This hold was settled, so it was never reversed.
  • No timer fired. Six timers were started:
    • keypad,
    • bank,
    • lift,
    • fueling,
    • idle,
    • finishing.
    Each one was cancelled by the event it was waiting for. The timer that clears a finished screen never started, because Idle is a resting state that keeps the receipt up for the next driver.
  • No queue. The bank answered both calls, so the pending list was written and emptied in the same breath. It exists for the day the line is down at the hang-up.
  • No clamp. The authority checked a $30 slip against $500 and 50 gallon limits and had nothing to lower. The clamp is for the day the station is lying.
  • No fault. The flow switch, the pulser, the breakaway, and the tank all reported normal on every tick.
